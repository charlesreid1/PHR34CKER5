# 2600 Hz

The tone that named a magazine.

## The frequency
2600 Hz was the single-frequency (SF) supervision tone used on AT&T's
long-distance trunks. Presence of 2600 Hz meant "this trunk is idle."
Absence meant "seized, billing." Toggle it at the right moment and you
could convince the far end to drop billing while keeping the trunk up.

## Cap'n Crunch
John T. Draper — "Captain Crunch" — is the name most associated with 2600 Hz,
after the discovery that a plastic whistle packaged in Cap'n Crunch cereal
boxes in the late 1960s emitted a near-perfect 2600 Hz when one hole was
covered.

## Why it worked, why it doesn't
In-band SF supervision meant the *voice path* and the *control path* were
the same wire. Common Channel Interoffice Signaling (CCIS) — and later SS7 —
moved control to a completely separate network. Once that migration was
complete (largely by the late 1980s in the US), whistling 2600 Hz into a
receiver did exactly nothing.
